TITLE: Hypoxia: your body at altitude
SUBJECT: 040 Human Performance
SUMMARY: Why the percentage of oxygen doesn't change but its partial pressure does, the four types of hypoxia, symptoms, time of useful consciousness, cabin altitude, and hyperventilation.
KEY: Air is about 78% nitrogen and 21% oxygen at every altitude. The partial pressure of oxygen falls with total pressure (Dalton's law).
KEY: About half the sea-level pressure at 18,000 ft, so about half the oxygen partial pressure.
KEY: Hypoxic (altitude), anaemic (blood can't carry: carbon monoxide, blood loss), stagnant (poor circulation: g forces, cold), histotoxic (cells can't use it: alcohol, poisons).
KEY: Judgement is affected first, often with euphoria. Night vision can deteriorate from about 5,000 ft.
KEY: Time of useful consciousness, roughly: FL250 3-5 min, FL300 1-2 min, FL350 30-60 s, FL400 15-20 s. Shorter after a rapid decompression.
KEY: Cabin altitude max 8,000 ft in normal operation. If in doubt between hypoxia and hyperventilation, use oxygen.

K: Second, anaemic hypoxia, sometimes called hypaemic. The air is fine, but the blood can't carry enough oxygen. Carbon monoxide is the classic cause, because it binds to haemoglobin far more readily than oxygen does. Smoking, a leaking cabin heater, blood loss and anaemia all fit here too.
T: So carbon monoxide means anaemic.
K: Yes. Third, stagnant hypoxia. The blood carries enough oxygen, but it isn't circulating properly. For example, under high g forces, or when your arms and legs are very cold.
K: And fourth, histotoxic hypoxia. The oxygen is delivered, but the cells can't use it. Alcohol, and certain poisons such as cyanide, cause this.

Early symptoms include impaired judgement, slower thinking, poor coordination, and often a feeling of euphoria, a sense that everything is fine. Then come headache, dizziness, tingling, tunnel vision, and a blue tinge to the lips and fingernails, called cyanosis. And eventually, unconsciousness.

T: Now, I've heard of something called the time of useful consciousness.
K: The time of useful consciousness is the time you have, after losing your oxygen supply, during which you can still act effectively, like putting on your oxygen mask. It's not the time until you pass out. It's the time until you stop being useful.
T: How long is it?
K: It falls steeply with altitude. Typical figures are around three to five minutes at twenty-five thousand feet, one to two minutes at thirty thousand, thirty to sixty seconds at thirty-five thousand, and only about fifteen to twenty seconds at forty thousand feet.

T: What about hyperventilation? I've heard it gets confused with hypoxia.
K: It does. Hyperventilation is breathing too fast or too deeply, often because of stress or anxiety. You blow off too much carbon dioxide, and you get dizziness, tingling in the fingers and around the lips, and a strange feeling of unreality. Those symptoms overlap with hypoxia.
T: So how do I tell them apart in the air?
K: At altitude, you often can't, reliably. So the safe rule is: if in doubt, treat it as hypoxia, and use oxygen. If it turns out to be hyperventilation, consciously slowing your breathing will help, and so will talking, because talking naturally slows your breathing down.
